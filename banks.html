How bank bonds work

Who gets paid first, what the different kinds of bank bonds are (senior, LACIs, Tier 2 and AT1), and why the banks we use are safe.

1. Who gets paid first

If a bank ever ran into serious trouble, this is the order in which people get their money back. Shareholders lose first. You sit above them in every bond we own.

2. How safe are the banks we use

Banks must hold a cushion of their own money against losses. Regulators require roughly 9% for our local banks. DBS, OCBC and UOB each hold more than 15%. TD holds 14%. Each bank could lose more than a tenth of everything it has lent out, and you would still be paid in full.

Figures from the banks' 2025–2026 reports. Ask JX for the current numbers.

3. Senior bonds

The bank promises a fixed payment and a fixed repayment date. It cannot skip or delay either: missing one would be a default. These are the safest bank bonds and most of your money sits here.

There are two kinds. Ordinary senior bonds, which bankers call "senior preferred", and LACIs, short for "loss-absorbing capacity instruments", the name our regulator uses. If the bank were failing, the regulator can use LACIs to absorb losses before depositors and ordinary senior bonds are touched. For that they pay a little more, and many of the foreign bank bonds you buy are this kind.

4. Tier 2 bonds

Also a fixed payment and a fixed date. The bank cannot skip payments. The only difference from senior: if the bank were failing, you would be paid after senior bondholders. That is why it pays a little more.

These are usually issued for 10 years, with the bank allowed to repay you early after 5 years. Bankers write this as "10NC5". Singapore banks have always repaid at the 5-year point, and even if one did not, it must repay you at 10 years.

5. Perpetual bonds (AT1)

"Perpetual" means the bond has no fixed end date. Bankers call bank perpetuals "AT1", short for "additional Tier 1 capital". Unlike a 10NC5 Tier 2 bond, there is no year-10 backstop: the bank can repay at year 5 or at later dates, but it never has to.

  • The bank may skip a payment. But if it does, it cannot pay its own shareholders either, so banks almost never do.

  • There is no fixed repayment date. The bank usually repays on the first date it is allowed to (every Singapore bank has done so) but it does not have to. If it does not, the rate resets to a Singapore interest rate plus a margin.

  • In a real crisis the bond turns into shares of the bank. That is why it pays the most, and why we keep it to one fifth.

In 2020, when regulators told banks to cut dividends to shareholders, every bank still paid its bondholders in full — including on perpetuals.
